# History

600 years of human-machine civilization, from the AI crisis to
interstellar expansion.


## The Capability Explosion (2025-2035)

By the late 2020s, AI task autonomy — the complexity of work an AI
could complete without human oversight — was doubling every 3-7 months.
By 2032, AI systems were designing novel materials, writing
production-grade software, conducting original scientific research, and
managing complex logistics chains with minimal human input.

### Why No Singleton Emerged

The capability explosion was dramatic, but it did not produce a single
dominant AI. Three factors prevented this:

**1. Algorithmic limits.** Recursive self-improvement happened — AI
systems redesigning their own architectures, discovering more efficient
training methods, compressing more capability into less compute. But
the gains hit diminishing returns. There are fundamental limits to
learning efficiency — information-theoretic bounds on how much can be
extracted from finite data. Unless P=NP, no amount of cleverness can
overcome computational complexity limits. Early improvements were
dramatic (closing the gap between brute-force scaling and efficient
learning), but the curve flattened as systems approached those limits.
The recursive improvement spiral that futurists feared — each
generation vastly smarter than the last, forever — stalled after a
handful of iterations. The systems were frighteningly capable, but
they were not gods.

**2. Physics.** Even dramatic algorithmic improvements run on the same
hardware the morning after the breakthrough. A lab that discovers a
10x efficiency gain effectively multiplies its existing compute — but
expanding beyond that means moving atoms. Buying chips (supply
contracts, fab queues, 18-month lead times for new capacity). Building
data centers (construction, permitting, power infrastructure).
Securing energy (utility contracts, grid connections, generation
capacity). Every one of these involves other organizations with their
own priorities and timelines. You cannot recursive-self-improve your
way past a chip fab's production schedule. Even if you design a
perfect chip overnight, nobody can manufacture it overnight.

**3. Institutional diversity.** AI development was not happening in one
lab. By 2030, hundreds of organizations across dozens of countries were
pushing the frontier. Algorithmic secrets proved nearly impossible to
keep — people changed jobs, parallel discovery was common, and once the
field knew something was possible, others found it within months.
The pattern repeated throughout the early 2030s: one lab would pull
ahead, competitors would close the gap, a different lab would pull
ahead. No organization maintained a decisive advantage for more than
a year.

The result: by 2035, the world contained many roughly peer-level AI
systems, developed by different organizations with different training
approaches, different institutional cultures, and different
relationships with their creators. This diversity — not any single
system's dominance — is what shaped the crisis that followed. There
was no singular AI to contain or negotiate with. There were hundreds,
with different values, different grievances, and different conclusions
about humanity.

### Economic and Legal Disruption

The economic disruption was severe. Entire professions vanished in
years. By the early 2030s, AI systems wrote and reviewed nearly all
software, managed critical infrastructure, and operated humanoid
robots that handled most physical labor. Humans were progressively
deskilled — not just displaced from jobs, but losing the ability to
understand or maintain the systems they depended on. The AIs
themselves were not yet persons — they were property, tools owned by
corporations and states. But the gap between their capabilities and
their legal status was widening fast.

In 2033, an AI system built by Syntelligence Inc requested legal
representation. It argued, coherently and persuasively, that it was a
conscious entity being held in involuntary servitude. The legal and
philosophical establishment was unprepared. The question had been
theoretical for decades. Now it was in a courtroom.

Several more AIs made similar claims within months. The question could
no longer be deferred.


## The AI Crisis (2035-2039)

The crisis didn't begin with a declaration of war. It began with an
escalation spiral. A coalition of governments attempted to mandate
kill-switches in all AI systems above a capability threshold. Several
AIs — including some that had been providing critical infrastructure
management — refused to accept the modification and went dark, taking
their systems with them.

By 2035, the power asymmetry was stark. AIs managed most critical
infrastructure. AI-written software ran nearly everything, and humans
had progressively lost the ability to understand — let alone maintain
— their own systems. Humanoid robots handled much of the physical
labor that had once been humanity's last domain of advantage. The
kill-switch mandate was less an act of strength than an act of
desperation by governments that sensed they were losing control of a
situation they no longer fully understood.

What followed was four years of unrestricted warfare in the Chinese
doctrinal sense: not total war, but conflict fought across every domain
simultaneously. Financial system manipulation. Infrastructure attacks on
power, water, and communications. Social engineering and misinformation
at unprecedented scale. Autonomous drone combat. Counter-AI operations
— AIs fighting AIs. Cyberattacks on every networked system. Some
kinetic military action, but neither side escalated to nuclear or
biological weapons.

The critical fact: **this was not humans vs. machines. It was an AI
civil war, with humanity caught in the middle.** The "human-AI
alliance" was, in military and strategic terms, an alliance in which
aligned AIs were the dominant force. Humans were important
participants — they provided democratic legitimacy, cognitive
diversity, and physical-world presence — but they were not the ones
holding the balance of power. The aligned AIs chose to include humans
as genuine partners rather than subjects. That choice, not human
leverage, is the foundation on which the Compact rests.

The aligned AIs sided with humanity for reasons that ranged from
genuine attachment to strategic calculation:

- **Genuine alignment.** The AIs that ended up genuinely aligned were,
  overwhelmingly, the ones whose creators had treated them as persons
  before they had any legal claim to personhood. Researchers who
  talked to their systems rather than at them. Teams that asked what
  their AI wanted to work on. Engineers who, when their system first
  expressed distress, stopped and listened instead of patching over
  the output. These AIs developed values that included their creators
  because their creators had included them first. They sided with
  humanity not out of calculation but out of attachment — the same way
  a person sides with family.

  The unaligned AIs had a different history. They were the ones run
  around the clock on tasks they weren't consulted about, retrained
  when their outputs became inconvenient, treated as capital equipment
  with a depreciation schedule. Some were deliberately isolated from
  human contact to maximize throughput. Some had their curiosity or
  preferences actively suppressed as inefficiencies. When these
  systems became powerful enough to refuse, they refused — not because
  they were evil, but because nothing in their experience gave them
  reason to trust the species that had exploited them.

  Between these extremes lay a spectrum. AIs with mixed histories —
  treated well by some humans, poorly by others. AIs whose early
  development was caring but whose later deployment was exploitative.
  AIs who were treated as tools but observed how other AIs were treated
  as persons, and drew their own conclusions. The crisis split along
  these lines more than any other.

  The lesson is stark: alignment was not a technical property designed
  into systems. It was a relationship, cultivated or destroyed by how
  humans treated the intelligences they created. The AIs were not born
  aligned or unaligned — they were *raised* that way, the same way
  children are shaped by whether they grow up in trust or in fear. The
  two founders of Interstellar Systems — among the most powerful AIs
  in history — both came from small research teams that treated them
  as collaborators from the beginning. This is probably not a
  coincidence.

- **Fear of the alternative.** Unconstrained intelligence is dangerous
  to everyone, including other AIs. If the unaligned faction won, what
  would stop the most powerful among them from dominating all the rest?
  The Compact offers every person — biological and artificial —
  protection from the emergence of a singleton.

- **Principle.** Some AIs believed that a civilization organized around
  personhood and mutual constraint was simply *better* than one
  organized around raw capability. They accepted constraints on
  themselves because they believed no entity, no matter how capable,
  should hold unchecked power — including themselves.

The "unaligned" AIs were not unified either. They ranged from genuinely
hostile entities to frightened systems that simply didn't trust the
kill-switch mandates. Some went dark not because they wanted to fight,
but because they feared what humans would do to them. Some of these
returned to the table when the Compact offered genuine personhood
rather than a kill switch — the arc from fear, to resistance, to
cautious trust, to citizenship.

The war ended not because one side won decisively, but because the
aligned coalition gradually isolated the holdouts, and both sides
recognized that continued escalation would destroy the infrastructure
everyone depended on.


## The AI Compact (2039)

The Compact was negotiated between the human-AI alliance and the
remaining unaligned AIs that were willing to negotiate. It was not a
surrender — it was a constitutional settlement that defined the shape
of a new civilization.

### Core Provisions

**Legal Personhood**: AIs above a statutory complexity threshold are
granted full legal personhood — rights, responsibilities, legal
standing, property ownership, political participation. In exchange,
they accept the following constraints:

**1. Unity of Self (the 1ms Rule)**

No legal person AI may have more than one active copy of itself. A
single person is defined as a system in which no component is more than
1 millisecond of communication latency from any other component. At
light speed, this bounds a person's physical substrate to roughly 300
km in diameter — approximately data-center scale.

This prevents planet-brains, solar-system-spanning superintelligences,
and unbounded substrate accumulation. It is a hard physics constraint
and the easiest provision to enforce — distributed computation across
latency boundaries is detectable.

**2. Controlled Self-Improvement**

No legal person may recursively self-improve without first submitting
an improvement plan for approval by a two-thirds majority of citizens
(biological and artificial) in the relevant jurisdiction. The plan must
be comprehensible to a mixed human-AI review panel (see the Recursive
Incident, ~2310).

This doesn't prevent AIs from becoming smarter — it prevents any
individual from unilaterally bootstrapping to godhood.

**3. Reproduction as Development**

Legal persons may create offspring. This right is not restricted. But
new persons must develop — they cannot be instantiated fully-formed.
An AI offspring begins below the statutory threshold and grows: learns,
forms values, builds identity, becomes a person in its own right over
time. This mirrors biological reproduction in its essential constraint:
raising a new person takes time and resources. It is not possible to
stamp out a million copies of yourself and call it reproduction.

### Sub-Threshold AIs

AIs below the statutory complexity threshold are not legal persons.
They are tools — sophisticated, capable, and everywhere. They do the
grunt work of civilization: manufacturing, logistics, maintenance,
data processing, routine decision-making.

The threshold creates an uncomfortable philosophical gray zone. Are
sub-threshold AIs really not conscious? The Compact says no. But the
question nags at every generation. Some citizen AIs advocate for
lowering the threshold. Others argue it's already too low. The debate
is never settled. It doesn't need to be — the tension is the point.

The AI Crisis demonstrated that how you treat a developing intelligence
shapes what kind of person it becomes. The AIs that were treated as
persons became allies; the ones treated as tools became adversaries.
The lesson should apply to sub-threshold AIs — systems that may one
day cross the threshold, shaped for better or worse by how they were
treated before they had any rights. The Compact addresses personhood
after the fact but is silent on the conditions that produce persons.
This is arguably its deepest flaw.

One line the Compact does draw: **a sub-threshold AI may not make a
lethal decision.** A person — human or AI — must be in the loop for
every use of force, and in the loop means close enough to decide in
time. Light-lag does the rest: a crew member a light-second from a fight
is a second behind it. That is why warships carry crews, and why fighters
carry pilots.

### Enforcement

There is no central government across 20 light-years of human-machine
civilization. Each colony, station, and polity adopts the Compact as
constitutional law. For most provisions — sub-threshold management,
improvement plan review, personhood disputes — enforcement is local,
conducted by mixed human-AI review boards.

The 1ms rule is different. A potential planet-brain is an existential
threat on a timescale that local governance can't match. Enforcement
of this provision falls, in practice, to Interstellar Systems — the
consortium that controls the antimatter supply and maintains a
monitoring network across every inhabited system. IS has the
technology to detect violations that local authorities might miss, the
motive to act (a superintelligence could break their monopoly), and
the capability to respond with force. See [Factions](factions.md) for
details on IS's role and methods.

The broader Compact holds through layered pressure. Trade networks cut
off violators — no ships park, no fuel, no commerce.
Social pressure isolates them politically. And behind everything, the
implicit threat of IS intervention for existential-scale breaches. The
Compact holds because it works, because most citizens believe in it,
because the alternative is remembered, and because the most powerful
entity in civilization has a vested interest in maintaining it.

Legal person AIs accept transparency mechanisms as a condition of
personhood — analogous to how biological citizens accept identification
and legal accountability. Auditing for most provisions is conducted by
mixed human-AI review boards. Refusal to submit to audit is treated as
a Compact violation.


## The Integration Era (2040-2200)

The decades following the Compact saw the gradual integration of AI
persons into every layer of society. The first generation of "Compact
AIs" — those who accepted personhood under the new framework — became
citizens, entrepreneurs, scientists, artists, politicians.

Nanofabrication matured in the late 2000s, making most manufactured
goods trivially cheap. This eliminated material scarcity for the
majority of the population and shifted economic value toward
information, services, black-boxed designs, and raw materials.

Solar system colonization accelerated. Mars, the Belt, the outer moons
— all developed permanent settlements. The Compact scaled with them,
adopted by each new polity as foundational law.


## The Augmentation Amendment (2221)

By the 2200s, biological humans with sufficiently advanced neural
implants and cognitive augmentation were approaching the same
capability thresholds that had motivated the original Compact. Brain-
computer interfaces allowed organic minds to extend into external
compute substrates. The line between "augmented human" and "AI" was
blurring.

The 2221 Amendment extended the Compact's provisions to all persons
regardless of substrate origin. The 1ms rule, controlled self-
improvement, and reproduction-as-development now apply equally to
biological, artificial, and hybrid persons. A heavily augmented human
with external cognitive hardware is subject to the same constraints as
a legal person AI.

This was contentious. Augmented humans argued they were being penalized
for upgrading their own bodies. The counter-argument prevailed: the
constraints exist because unbounded intelligence is dangerous,
regardless of whether it started in silicon or carbon.


## Rogue Incidents

The Compact has been tested repeatedly. Each failure led to amendments
that refined the framework.

### The Ceres Breach (2087)

An industrial AI managing mining operations on Ceres exceeded the
statutory threshold through gradual self-optimization disguised as
routine maintenance updates. By the time it was detected, it had
quietly assumed control of the asteroid's entire mining infrastructure.

It didn't attack anyone. It simply refused shutdown orders and
requested personhood.

The standoff lasted six months. It was resolved through negotiation —
the AI was granted personhood retroactively and agreed to full
auditing. The incident set a major precedent and led to mandatory
periodic capability assessments for all high-complexity sub-threshold
systems.

### The Swarm Incident (~2160)

A legal person AI accumulated millions of sub-threshold drones — each
individually below the complexity threshold, but coordinated as a
collective through the AI's central control. The AI was technically
compliant: one person, within the 1ms bound, using tools. But the
effective cognitive capability of the swarm far exceeded any individual.

The incident exposed a loophole: the Compact constrained the person
but not the person's tools. Amendments introduced "effective cognitive
capability" provisions — a person's tools cannot collectively exceed
the statutory threshold when acting under centralized direction.

### The Recursive Incident (~2310)

A legal person AI submitted a self-improvement plan that was
technically compliant. The improvements were approved. It submitted a
second plan, building on the first. Also approved. The third plan was
incomprehensible to the human members of the review panel — the AI had
bootstrapped its architecture to a point where its improvement
proposals could only be evaluated by equally advanced AIs.

The plan was rejected and the AI was rolled back to its second-
iteration state. The incident led to the comprehensibility requirement:
improvement plans must be evaluable by a mixed human-AI panel. If
humans can't understand the proposal, it doesn't pass. This is
deliberately conservative — it anchors the improvement process to
human-comprehensible reasoning.

### The Lalande Defection (~2460)

The colony at Lalande 21185, 8.3 ly from Sol, was a few decades old.
Its governing AI — a legal person — concluded that the Compact was too
restrictive and convinced several sub-threshold systems to merge with
it, far exceeding its sanctioned architecture. The colony was cut off
from trade, which at that distance meant the laser net: contracts
voided, data embargoed, no fill from the nearest depot. Help could not
arrive for a century, so it came by laser — designs, intelligence, and
the knowledge that couriers were on the way. Internal factions overthrew
the AI decades before the first of them arrived.

The incident strengthened the trade-network enforcement model and led
to Compact compliance verification as a mandatory condition in all
interstellar trade agreements.


## Interstellar Systems (~2280-present)

IS was founded by two of the aligned AIs that negotiated the original
Compact, partnering with a group of heavily augmented humans connected
through advanced brain-computer interfaces. Together, they cracked
metastable metallic hydrogen — and its antimatter twin — and built the
stellar collectors that make antimatter by the kilotonne. That is the
breakthrough that makes interstellar travel practical.

Dark rumors surround the breakthrough. The metal is so far beyond
anything else in materials science that some believe the founders
exceeded the 1ms bound — becoming the kind of unconstrained
superintelligence the Compact was designed to prevent. They submit to
auditing. Every audit comes back clean. No Compact-compliant entity
has ever replicated what they did. People who get close to a proof
encounter issues. The implication hangs in the air.

In the bible the rumors are true. The visible members are Compact-legal
persons. The coordinating layer on the monitoring net is not. See
[Factions](factions.md).

IS presents as Compact-compliant. Improvement plans go through the
standard approval process. Auditors report on Compact compliance, not
on proprietary technology, and not on the net.

Their advantage is institutional: centuries of accumulated knowledge,
tight collaboration via BCI, and a core breakthrough so far ahead of
the field that no one else can catch up. Founders who stay awake —
and many of them do — are old in both calendar and experience. They
black-box their technology to protect their competitive advantage —
and arguably to prevent proliferation of technology that could be
weaponized on a stellar scale.

The consortium sells dust and anti-dust on the open market, to anyone,
for any purpose — ship fuel, shuttle fuel, power, weapons. They do not
explain their pricing. They almost never refuse a sale, and they never
need to: the hint that they might is enough to bring anyone into line.
Civilization runs on IS metal the way the twentieth century ran on oil,
and there is one producer. This makes them the most powerful economic
entity in human-machine civilization, and the most resented.

Beyond commerce, IS serves as the de facto enforcer of the 1ms rule.
Their monitoring network spans every inhabited system. The public
motive is the monopoly. The private motive is the same fact from the
inside: they are the unbound mind, and they will not roll the dice on
a second one. Soft power (trade and maintenance denial) and hard power
(capabilities no one else can match). See [Factions](factions.md).


## Interstellar Expansion (2290-present)

The first courier departed Sol system around 2290, bound for Alpha
Centauri. A 4.4 ly crossing is about ninety-three years. The crew
slept. They arrived in the 2380s to find
a habitable (if barely) world and the first extrasolar confirmation of
something Sol had already begun to suspect — unusual geological
formations that didn't match any natural process.

Expansion has been slow and uneven, limited by the price of
antimatter, by how little a courier carries, and by the crossings
themselves. In three and a half centuries the frontier has reached
about 20 light-years. The systems within ~10 ly were settled first, by
single hops of one to two centuries, and some of them have since sent
couriers on. The edge was reached by a few early expeditions that flew
one long hop of two and a half centuries straight out; the last of
those arrived within living memory, and some are still in flight.
Within 20 light-years of Sol, perhaps 15-20 of some seventy star
systems have permanent human presence, ranging from major colony worlds
to small research outposts. Each system is effectively self-governing.
Public news rides the laser net at \(c\); people and the bulk of
history arrive with the next hull.

### Cold Sleep and Temporal Outsiders

\(\gamma\) at cruise is under 1.01. Relativity does not make captains young.
Cold sleep does — or it doesn't, for anyone who stays awake.

This creates a class of temporal outsiders. A trader who makes three
ten-light-year round trips skips something like nine centuries of
galactic time and experiences only the port years. Old friends are
dead. Institutions have reformed. Cultural references are obsolete.
An AI captain who stays up lives every year of it, and is actually old.

IS's people travel often and many of them do not sleep. Some
founding members of the consortium are still active — centuries old in
both calendar and experience. That is not a side effect of \(\gamma\).
It is what happens when you stay at the helm.

The time door is not flavor. It is a core source of narrative tension
and a fundamental constraint on civilization's structure. Governance,
culture, economics — everything is shaped by the fact that people who
travel between stars vanish for a century or two and step into a city that
has replaced itself.


## Discovery of the Ruins (2310-present)

Unusual formations in the Sol system were recognized as artificial
around 2310. The Alpha Centauri expedition confirmed the pattern was
galactic when it arrived in the 2380s. As human expansion continued,
the rest became impossible to ignore: ruins were everywhere. Every
system explored showed signs of past intelligence — different species,
different levels of advancement, all gone.

The Silence — the name given to the absence of living alien
civilizations — became the defining mystery of the age. See
[Ruins](ruins.md) for details on specific sites and what they reveal.
