# Interstellar Trade and Economics

## Why Trade Exists

Nanofabrication makes most manufactured goods trivially cheap. If you
have the design and the raw atoms, you can build anything. This should
eliminate trade — and locally, it does. Within a system, most goods
are effectively free.

Between star systems, trade exists because of what nanofab can't solve:

- **You can't fabricate atoms.** Nanofab rearranges matter; it doesn't
  create it. A system without gas giants has no local ³He. A system
  without an asteroid belt has no platinum. The raw elements must be
  imported.
- **You can't transmit people.** Both biological and AI persons must
  physically travel to relocate. AIs cannot transmit themselves without
  violating the Compact's single-instance rule, and the bandwidth of
  interstellar laser links is far too low for a person-scale data
  transfer regardless.
- **You can't transmit secrets.** Black-boxed technology — stardrives,
  advanced weapons, proprietary computing architectures — is never sent
  as data, even encrypted. Physical delivery only.
- **You can't transmit enough data.** Laser links between systems
  sustain megabits per second at best against the inverse square law. A
  ship carrying petabytes of physical storage is higher bandwidth for
  bulk transfers. Even Interstellar Systems moves its corporate records
  between facilities by ship — they don't trust the laser links, and the
  volume would saturate them for years.
- **You can't fabricate the unique.** Alien artifacts, original art,
  objects with provenance. These have value precisely because they are
  singular.


## What Crosses Between Stars

### Passengers and Persons

The bread-and-butter revenue for any ship operator. Biological humans
in cold sleep, AI persons in hardened storage, augmented humans with
their cognitive hardware. A hop is a century. Passage is emigration,
exile, colonization, or an institutional posting — not a sales call.
Everyone who needs to be in another system in another century pays, or
buys their own ship.

### Elemental Commodities

The atoms that nanofab needs but a system can't source locally.
Distribution depends on planetary system architecture — gas giants,
asteroid belts, rocky planets, or nothing much at all.

**Tier 1 — Universal demand, quasi-currency:**

| Element | Why it matters | Where it comes from |
|---------|---------------|-------------------|
| Helium-3 (³He) | D-³He fusion reactor fuel. Every ship, every station, every colony needs it. | Gas giant atmospheres. Systems without gas giants import or die slowly. |
| Platinum (Pt) | Catalysts, high-performance manufacturing, dense value store. The closest thing to a universal currency. | Differentiated asteroids. Systems with a good belt are rich; systems without are poor. |
These two function as interstellar quasi-currency. A trader accepts
payment in ³He or platinum because they know exactly where to sell it
at a profit.

**Tier 2 — Important, unevenly distributed:**

| Element | Why it matters | Where it comes from |
|---------|---------------|-------------------|
| Iridium (Ir) | Nearly as dense as osmium. Extreme hardness, corrosion resistance. High-performance structural applications. | Rare everywhere. Concentrated in asteroid impacts and differentiated bodies. |
| Rhodium (Rh) | Rarer than platinum. Irreplaceable catalytic properties. | Same as iridium — asteroid-rich systems. |
| Lithium (Li) | Reactor coolant loops, batteries, pharmaceuticals. Light, reactive, versatile. | Unevenly distributed in planetary crusts. Some worlds are lithium-rich; others have almost none. |
| Tungsten (W) | Highest melting point of any element (3,695 K). Everything built for extreme temperatures needs it. | Concentrated in specific geological formations. A system without tungsten-bearing ores imports it. |
| Neodymium (Nd) | NdFeB permanent magnets. Every motor, every actuator that has to stay a magnet when it is not a coil. | Rare earth — concentrated in specific mineral deposits. Not every system has accessible sources. |
| Dysprosium (Dy) | Stabilizes neodymium magnets at high temperature. Critical for anything that runs hot and magnetic. | Same as neodymium, even rarer. |

**Tier 3 — Bulk commodities, valuable only where locally scarce:**

Iron, nickel, copper, silicon. Absurd to ship across interstellar
distances under normal circumstances, but desperate systems exist.
A colony with no accessible asteroids or rocky bodies needs base metals
imported until it can establish its own extraction infrastructure.

### Black-Boxed Technology

Stardrives, advanced weapons systems, proprietary computing
architectures. Physically delivered, never transmitted. The most
valuable cargo by mass, and the most dangerous to carry.

### Bulk Data

A ship arriving from another system carries a complete data dump:
scientific archives, cultural media, market histories, updated star
charts, news. Even at megabit-per-second laser link rates, this volume
of data would take years to transmit. Physical transport is higher
bandwidth.

Recent market data from another system is immediately valuable to
anyone planning a trip there. A ship that just arrived from Tau Ceti
has the most current picture of Tau Ceti's economy, politics, and
conditions. Selling that information is a business in itself.

### Unique Objects

Alien artifacts, original art, objects with historical provenance.
These have value because they are singular — nanofab can copy the
atoms, but it can't copy the authenticity.


## The Interstellar Futures Market

### The Problem

You're loading cargo in System A, bound for System B. The journey
takes a century. You're betting on what System B will need when you
arrive, based on laser data that's already a decade stale and will be
a lifetime old when you dock. Other ships may be heading there with
the same cargo. The market may have shifted. The colony may have had
a disaster — or a boom — or several of each.

This is cathedral-funding, not a clipper run: institutions think in
dynasties, captains skip the years, and fortunes are made and lost on
information that was true when someone you will never meet wrote it
down.

### How It Works

Each system has a local currency and local economy. Between systems,
trade is coordinated through a futures market operating over high-
energy laser links.

**Contracts** specify:
- Commodity (or service — passage counts)
- Quantity
- Destination system
- Delivery window (a range of dates)
- Payment in destination-local currency, or in trade commodities
  (³He, platinum)

**Exclusive contracts**: One party takes the contract, one ship
fulfills it. Guaranteed payment on delivery. Higher margins, but you're
committed — the cargo bay is spoken for. If you're late, you breach.

**Non-exclusive contracts**: Open to anyone. First ship(s) to deliver
up to the contract quantity get paid. Everyone else arrives with a hold
full of cargo they need to find a buyer for — at whatever the local
spot price happens to be, which may be cratered by oversupply. Higher
potential reward, much higher risk.

### The Latency Problem

Contract postings propagate across laser links at lightspeed. A
contract posted at Tau Ceti arrives at Sol 12 years later. By the time
a trader at Sol sees it, loads cargo, and makes the journey, ~130 years
have passed at Tau Ceti. The contract may have been filled. The need
may have evaporated. The colony may not exist anymore. Exclusive
contracts are written with delivery windows measured in decades, and
only houses that expect to still be there sign them.

Smart traders don't rely solely on contract postings. They cultivate
information networks — buying recent data from arriving ships, building
models of each system's economic cycles, tracking which other ships
are heading where. The best traders are the ones with the best
information, not the biggest cargo holds.

### Settlement and Trust

There is no interstellar court. Contract enforcement relies on
reputation, propagated by the same ships that carry trade.

A colony that stiffs a trader on a contract: that information travels
with every ship that departs. Within years, every system within range
knows. That colony's contracts become worthless — no one delivers to a
port that doesn't pay. The economic isolation is devastating.

A captain who takes an exclusive contract and doesn't deliver: same
mechanism. Blacklisted across the network. A ship with a bad
reputation can't fill cargo bays or take passengers.

Reputation is the enforcement mechanism because reputation is the only
thing that propagates. It's slow, imperfect, and subject to
manipulation — but it's all there is.

### Insurance

Insurance makes interstellar trade possible. Without it, only the
wealthiest operators could absorb the risk of a lost ship or a
collapsed market. With it, smaller traders can take on cargoes that
would otherwise be ruinous to lose.

The model is Lloyd's of London — which itself started as a coffee
house where merchants shared risk on individual ships. At each major
port, underwriters (wealthy individuals, AI persons, colony investment
funds, or syndicates) take slips on ships and cargoes. A broker
circulates a description of the ship, cargo, route, captain's record,
and premium. Underwriters sign on for a percentage of the risk. No
single party carries the whole exposure.

**What's insurable:**

- **Hull**: The ship itself. Enormously expensive — a stardrive alone
  is priceless. Hull insurance premiums reflect IS maintenance status:
  a ship overdue for service pays vastly more, or is simply
  uninsurable. Underwriters enforce maintenance schedules through
  pricing without IS lifting a finger.
- **Cargo**: The value of goods in transit. The most common policy.
  Premiums vary by commodity (volatile goods cost more), route
  (dangerous routes cost more), and captain reputation.
- **Market risk**: The novel one. This is essentially a put option:
  "If ³He at Tau Ceti drops below X per tonne when I arrive, the
  underwriter covers the difference." The underwriter is betting they
  understand the destination economy better than the trader — using
  data that's years old on both sides. This is where the real money
  is made and lost.
- **Passage**: Covers passengers in cold sleep. A passenger who
  doesn't arrive is a claim. Families, employers, and colonies buy
  passage insurance on people they're sending.
- **Liability**: Covers damage to port facilities, other ships, cargo
  handling incidents. Required for docking at most major ports.

**The latency nightmare:**

If a ship is lost between systems, the underwriter doesn't know until
another ship carries the news — potentially a lifetime later. The
premium has been sitting in the underwriter's accounts, invested,
growing. Then a claim arrives for a loss that happened a century ago.

An underwriter with 50 ships insured, all departed generations ago,
must hold reserves against the possibility that any of them are already
lost. Managing reserves against that uncertainty — with claim arrivals
that are essentially random — requires deep pockets and sophisticated
actuarial modeling. This naturally favors large syndicates over
individual underwriters.

**Verification and fraud:**

Proving a loss that happened in deep space or at a system light-years
away is difficult. Ship AI telemetry is the primary evidence — if the
crew escapes in small craft, the logs exist. If the ship vanishes with
no survivors, the underwriter pays on presumed loss after a waiting
period (typically defined as twice the expected transit time with no
contact).

False claims — scuttling a ship, diverting cargo — would eventually
surface through the reputation network. But "eventually" might mean
a century. Insurance fraud is a long game, and some practitioners have
gotten away with it for entire (slept) careers. Premiums reflect this reality:
captains with long, clean records pay less. First-time operators pay
through the nose.

**Insurance as power:**

Underwriting syndicates are quiet power brokers. By pricing premiums,
they effectively decide which routes are viable, which captains can
trade, and which cargoes move. A syndicate that refuses to insure
shipments to a particular system makes that system harder to trade
with — not impossible, but only the bold or desperate go uninsured.

This creates a second enforcement layer alongside the reputation
network. A colony that's developed a bad reputation doesn't just lose
traders — it loses insurability. Underwriters won't cover shipments
there. The economic strangulation compounds.

### Cross-Currency Settlement

There is no universal currency. Each system's money is worth what that
system's economy backs it with. Between systems, trade is essentially
barter intermediated by local currencies:

1. Arrive at System A with cargo. Sell for System A credits.
2. Buy cargo (or commodities) with System A credits.
3. Travel to System B. Sell cargo for System B credits.
4. Repeat.

The balance of trade IS the currency exchange. Commodity quasi-
currencies (³He, platinum) smooth this — a trader who
doesn't want to speculate on cargo can convert to commodities that
hold value anywhere.

Historical parallel: exactly how East India Company trade worked.
Silver east, manufactured goods west, tea and spice back again. No
currency exchange — just goods moving in circles, with profit captured
at each conversion.

### Gameplay Tensions

- **Route planning**: Which systems need what? Where can you buy cheap
  and sell dear? Optimize your circuit.
- **Information trading**: Selling recent market data from systems
  you've visited. Your knowledge has a shelf life — it decays as time
  passes and conditions change.
- **Risk management**: Exclusive contracts are safe but constrain you.
  Non-exclusive contracts are a race. Diversified cargo hedges your
  bets but caps your upside.
- **Commodity banking**: Accepting payment in ³He or platinum instead
  of local currency, because you know where to sell it reliably.
- **The IS maintenance tax**: Your route must pass an Interstellar
  Systems service facility every ~60 light-years (~600 years of flight).
  This constrains your circuit and may force suboptimal legs.
- **Reputation management**: Every contract honored builds your name.
  Every breach — or association with a bad port — costs you.
- **Insurance decisions**: Insure the cargo and eat the premium, or
  go bare and keep the margin? Market risk insurance costs more but
  eliminates the worst downside. Hull insurance is non-negotiable if
  you ever want to dock at a major port again.
- **Underwriter relationships**: A good relationship with a syndicate
  means better rates and access to market intelligence the syndicate
  has gathered from other clients. Burning that relationship has
  lasting consequences.


## Interstellar Systems and the Shape of Civilization

Stardrive maintenance is required every ~60 light-years of travel
(nine to twelve centuries of flight at cruise — the interval is fluence
and wear, not a calendar).
Interstellar Systems performs all maintenance. They have service
facilities in perhaps 3-5 systems across inhabited space. The
inspection is opaque — they tell you what needs doing and what it
costs, never why. You pay, because the alternative is a stardrive
failure, and a drive that throws a million tonnes at five percent of
\(c\) does not fail quietly.

This gives IS control over the topology of human expansion:

**The 30 light-year leash.** A ship can travel at most ~30 light-years
from the nearest IS facility before it must turn back for maintenance —
about six centuries of flight. Effectively, civilization cannot expand
beyond 30 ly from an IS service point. IS chooses where to build
facilities. Therefore IS chooses the shape of the frontier.

**Service hub economics.** Systems with IS facilities become mandatory
waypoints for every ship. This makes them trade hubs, population
centers, and political powers — simply because every captain must dock
there eventually. IS doesn't need to govern these systems; their
presence does the work.

**The burn-the-boats option.** A colony expedition that goes beyond
30 ly from the nearest IS facility knows the stardrive will eventually
need maintenance it can't get. The drive will fail. This is a one-way
trip — permanent settlement with no return. Some colonies were founded
this way. Whether any survived is an open question.

**The fuel leash.** The drive burns only metallic hydrogen, and only
IS makes it. A full set of bands is one crossing. IS sells metal at
its facilities and at depots it stocks itself; it sets the price,
which is the largest line in any freight budget, and it decides which
systems get a depot. A system without one is not closed. It is slow:
ships must carry the fuel home, and out-and-back on one fill is
~0.026c with a full hold — half a freighter's speed, twice the
crossing. Pulling a depot is
IS's quiet embargo.

**Strategic refusal.** If IS declines to build a facility in a
particular direction, that direction is closed. Why they refuse — if
they ever do — is one of the most consequential questions in
civilization. Is it economics? Politics? Do they know something about
what's out there?


## Open Questions

- How many IS service facilities, and where?
- Has IS ever refused to service a ship? Under what circumstances?
- What's the spot price of ³He at different systems? Does it vary
  enough to be a meaningful trade opportunity?
- How many IS fuel depots, and where? What does metal cost, and does
  IS price it by system?
- Is there a resale market in dust — ship-to-ship transfers, a full
  compartment sold on? Does IS allow it?
- Are there black-market stardrive mechanics? (Almost certainly not —
  the technology is too far beyond anyone else. But rumors persist.)
- How does piracy interact with the futures market? Intercepting a ship
  is stealing not just cargo but potentially an exclusive contract.
- What happens when a colony can't pay for stardrive maintenance? Do
  they become indentured to IS? Does IS ever write off the debt?
